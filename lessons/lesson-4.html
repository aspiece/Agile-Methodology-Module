Vocabulary

Student Hub connection

Prioritize the Student Hub backlog

The Student Hub team now has more requests than it can finish at once. Some requests affect access or reliability. Others add convenience or visual polish.

Your job is to help the team put the strongest evidence first and explain why the order can change.

Use value, risk, effort, and dependency evidence to explain why one backlog item should move ahead of another.

Your prioritization decision should explain value, risk, effort, dependencies, and visibility so the team can understand why one backlog item deserves attention before another.

This reasoning helps the team avoid choosing work only because it is familiar, loud, or easy, rather than because it best supports the product goal.

When you defend backlog order, reference stakeholder impact, implementation uncertainty, accessibility risk, dependency management, and evidence from the visual board so the prioritization rationale is transparent.

Your first team challenge

The team can only move a few items into Ready. It needs a clear reason for which Student Hub items rise to the top.

📖 Your next move: Read the short explanation below. Look for how planning, feedback, and change work together.

A Product Backlog is an ordered list of work needed to improve a product. It is the single source of work for the Scrum Team.

Backlog items may describe features, fixes, research, or other needs. The Product Owner is accountable for Product Backlog management. This includes making items clear and ordering them to improve product value.

An ordered backlog is not a fixed promise. The team changes it as users give feedback and new risks appear. Teams may consider user value, urgency, risk, evidence, need, and effort.

For example, an access barrier may move above a color theme request because it blocks students from using the product. Effort matters, but the smallest item is not always the most valuable.

Prioritization and Visible Work

Estimated time: 11 minutes

▶️ Your next move: As you watch and read, look for how cards and columns help a team see what work is ready, active, blocked, or done.

What Is Kanban?

Show how Kanban boards, cards, and columns make work visible and help teams notice flow.

Open video in new tabOpen transcript

A team can use a simple board to make work visible. A priority board can show what is Ready, Next, or Later. A workflow board can show what is Ready, In Progress, Blocked, or Done.

The board helps people see the current order and discuss changes. It does not choose priorities for the team.

The Product Owner remains accountable for Product Backlog ordering. Kanban style boards are common Agile tools, but they are not required Scrum artifacts.

A minimum viable product, or MVP, is the smallest usable version of a product that can provide value and support learning. MVP is a common product planning term, not a required Scrum artifact. An MVP should still meet quality and access needs.

When evidence conflicts, ask which item creates the most value or reduces the greatest risk. Also consider who is affected and how strong the evidence is. Record a short reason for each high priority item so the decision can be reviewed later.

Step 4.4 · Practical application

Order the Student Hub Product Backlog

Estimated time: 15 minutes

Use evidence to order product work and make the decision visible.

  1. Choose the scenario that best matches your career program interest.
  2. Order the five backlog items from highest to lowest priority.
  3. Place the top two items in Ready and the others in Later.
  4. Write 3 to 4 sentences that defend your top three choices. Use user value, risk, need, evidence, and effort in your explanation.

Complete one career scenario. You may choose another afterward for optional practice.

Your selected program: Software Development

Situation: The Student Hub backlog includes a blocked keyboard date filter, a mobile calendar layout problem, announcement search, a dark theme, and an analytics page with no user evidence yet.

A strong response places the keyboard access fix near the top because it blocks use. It also uses evidence to compare value, risk, and effort instead of ranking by preference alone.

Your selected program: Cybersecurity

Situation: The Student Hub backlog includes automatic sign out on shared devices, clearer password reset messages, a profile picture option, announcement search, and an admin report with no security evidence yet.

A strong response places account protection work near the top when shared device risk is clear. It explains how security risk, user impact, and evidence shape the order.

Your selected program: Data Science

Situation: The Student Hub backlog includes fixing stale dashboard data, adding a last updated label, adding chart colors, creating a teacher export, and building a prediction feature with no tested need yet.

A strong response prioritizes data accuracy and freshness because students may make decisions from the dashboard. It uses evidence and risk before visual polish.

Your selected program: Artificial Intelligence

Situation: The Student Hub backlog includes checking AI study helper summaries for missing assignment details, adding a confidence notice, changing the helper icon, adding course filters, and testing a new chatbot with no user evidence yet.

A strong response prioritizes accurate and safe AI support before cosmetic changes. It explains how user trust, error risk, and evidence affect the backlog order.

Your selected program: Game Development

Situation: The Student Hub backlog includes remappable controls for a practice game, clearer score feedback, a new badge design, faster loading, and a leaderboard with no privacy review yet.

A strong response places access, playability, and privacy risk above cosmetic changes. It uses evidence about who is blocked and what value the item creates.

Your selected program: Physical Computing

Situation: The Student Hub backlog includes clearer out of range sensor alerts, a sensor status message, a new dashboard color, device setup instructions, and a remote control feature with no safety review yet.

A strong response prioritizes safety and clear device status before visual polish or unreviewed control features. It connects the order to risk, evidence, and user value.
