# Agile Module Project Context

## Project Goal

Build a polished, asynchronous, eight-lesson Agile Methodology module for high school students enrolled in Introduction to Software Engineering & Career Essentials.

This module is not intended to make students Agile experts. It should give them enough conceptual understanding and practice that they can successfully use a lightweight Agile process during later software-development projects.

## Instructional Philosophy

The module should emphasize **doing and decision-making** more than terminology.

Students should repeatedly answer questions such as:

- What does the user actually need?
- What should the team build first?
- What can reasonably fit into this sprint?
- What is blocking progress?
- Who should respond to this problem?
- What did stakeholders say about the product?
- What should the team change next?
- How could the team improve its process?

The application portion of each lesson should remain relatively simple because students will get authentic practice later when they build real software.

## Proposed Narrative

Students follow a fictional Agile software team developing **Student Hub**, a school-focused web application.

The product begins as a broad client request and becomes more defined as the module progresses.

Possible initial client brief:

> A school wants a digital Student Hub that makes it easier for students to find assignments, events, announcements, resources, and important account information.

Possible stakeholders:
- students,
- teachers,
- school administrators,
- technical support staff.

Possible backlog items:
- reliable login,
- password reset,
- assignment calendar,
- school announcements,
- upcoming events,
- mobile-responsive layout,
- event reminders,
- search,
- favorites,
- profile customization,
- dark mode,
- improved error messages.

Use functional, accessibility, reliability, and user-experience needs to make prioritization decisions meaningful.

## Scenario Progression

### Lesson 1
Students learn why Agile is useful when requirements change.

Example:
The client changes an important requirement after development has begun.

### Lesson 2
Students meet the fictional Scrum Team and identify responsibilities.

### Lesson 3
Stakeholders provide needs and requests.
Students convert them into user stories and acceptance criteria.

### Lesson 4
The Product Backlog becomes too large to complete at once.
Students prioritize based on evidence.

### Lesson 5
The team has limited sprint capacity.
Students select work and establish a Sprint Goal.

### Lesson 6
During development, a blocker appears.
Students analyze the team's Daily Scrum and determine what should happen next.

### Lesson 7
The team demonstrates its increment.
Stakeholders provide feedback and a changed requirement.
Students determine how the Product Backlog should adapt and complete a short retrospective.

### Lesson 8
Students see the full process together, solve one final Agile scenario, and complete the end-of-module assessment.

## Future Development Project Connection

After this module, students should encounter familiar Agile tools during their real programming assignments.

The teacher should be able to say:

- Create your user stories.
- Build your Product Backlog.
- Establish your Sprint Goal.
- Choose your Sprint Backlog.
- Update your task board.
- Identify blockers.
- Demonstrate your increment.
- Complete your retrospective.

These directions should feel familiar because students practiced each one during the module.

## Preferred Assessment Philosophy

Use frequent low-stakes checks.

Questions should increasingly move from:

“What is a Product Backlog?”

toward:

“A development team has 18 possible features and bug fixes. The Product Owner orders them according to value, risk, and current needs. Which Scrum artifact is being updated?”

Use realistic scenarios.

Multiple-choice distractors should:
- all be plausible,
- be similar in length,
- avoid obvious joke answers,
- avoid revealing the correct answer through detail or wording.

Use manual grading sparingly.

Good open-ended tasks include:
- one user story,
- one Sprint Goal,
- one prioritization rationale,
- one short retrospective.

## Accuracy Notes

Use current, accurate Scrum terminology.

Important distinctions:

- Agile is broader than Scrum.
- Scrum is an Agile framework.
- Scrum Master does not manage or supervise Developers as a boss.
- Product Owner is accountable for maximizing product value and effective Product Backlog management.
- Developers determine how to accomplish the work.
- User stories are a common Agile practice but are not a required Scrum artifact.
- The Sprint Goal is the Sprint's commitment.
- The Sprint Backlog can change as Developers learn more.
- A Daily Scrum is for Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt their plan.
- Sprint Review inspects product progress with stakeholders and informs future adaptation.
- Sprint Retrospective focuses on improving quality and team effectiveness.
