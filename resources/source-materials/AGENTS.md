# AGENTS.md

## Project Purpose

This project is building an **8-lesson asynchronous Agile Methodology module** for high school students in an **Introduction to Software Engineering & Career Essentials** course.

The module is foundational. Students will learn Agile concepts asynchronously, then apply those concepts later during their actual software-development projects.

Do not turn the Agile module itself into a large software-development project. Application activities should be short, realistic, and scenario-based.

## Audience

- High school students, grades 9–12
- Introductory Software Engineering students
- Career Essentials is embedded throughout the module
- Assume students are new to Agile/Scrum terminology

Use student-friendly language without oversimplifying concepts into inaccuracies.

## Instructional Model

Each lesson should generally follow this structure:

1. **Learn** — concise reading and/or short instructional video
2. **Check** — short, mostly auto-graded knowledge check
3. **Apply** — realistic software-development scenario or small artifact
4. **Connect** — brief connection to how students will use the concept in future development projects

Avoid unnecessary written responses. Use open-ended work only when students need to demonstrate authentic thinking, such as:
- writing a user story,
- establishing a Sprint Goal,
- prioritizing work,
- explaining a software-team decision,
- or completing a retrospective.

Assessments should favor **application and scenario-based questions** over simple vocabulary recall.

Distractors in multiple-choice questions should be plausible and similar in length so students cannot easily guess based on formatting.

## Core Design Principle

Students should:

**Learn Agile → practice it briefly in fictional software scenarios → apply it authentically during later development projects.**

Do not make students practice Agile by treating ordinary homework completion as a fake software sprint unless there is a clear instructional reason.

## Continuous Scenario

Whenever practical, use one fictional software project across the module rather than unrelated examples.

Working concept:

### Student Hub

A school is developing a web application to help students access:
- assignments,
- events,
- announcements,
- school resources,
- account features,
- and other student services.

Students do not need to actually program Student Hub during the Agile module.

Instead, they make decisions for the fictional development team:
- interpret stakeholder needs,
- write user stories,
- prioritize backlog items,
- plan a sprint,
- respond to blockers,
- process stakeholder feedback,
- and improve the team's process.

The scenario should evolve across lessons.

## Recommended 8-Lesson Sequence

### Lesson 1 — Introduction to Agile
Teach:
- what Agile is,
- why software teams use it,
- iterative development,
- flexibility,
- customer collaboration,
- responding to change,
- Agile values,
- Agile vs. traditional sequential development.

Application:
Students analyze a changing software-project scenario and determine why an Agile approach would help.

### Lesson 2 — Scrum & the Agile Team
Teach:
- Scrum as an Agile framework,
- Product Owner,
- Scrum Master,
- Developers,
- stakeholders,
- self-management,
- team accountability,
- blockers/impediments.

Important:
The Scrum Master is **not the team's boss or manager**.

Application:
Students determine how different Scrum Team members should respond to realistic development situations.

### Lesson 3 — User Stories & Requirements
Teach:
- understanding users,
- requirements,
- user stories,
- acceptance criteria,
- translating a user need into software work.

User story format:

**As a [type of user], I want [goal/feature] so that [reason/benefit].**

Application:
Students convert stakeholder requests into user stories and simple acceptance criteria.

### Lesson 4 — Product Backlog & Prioritization
Teach:
- Product Backlog,
- backlog items,
- ordering/prioritization,
- Product Owner accountability,
- value,
- effort/estimates at an introductory level,
- changing requirements,
- backlog refinement,
- MVP where useful.

Application:
Students prioritize a fictional software backlog using evidence about user needs, risks, and project goals.

### Lesson 5 — Sprints & Sprint Planning
Teach:
- Sprint,
- Sprint Planning,
- Sprint Goal,
- Sprint Backlog,
- limited team capacity,
- selecting work,
- breaking work into tasks,
- simple estimation,
- dependencies.

Application:
Students select work for a short fictional sprint and create a simple Sprint Goal and Sprint Plan.

Important:
Focus the commitment on the **Sprint Goal**. The Sprint Backlog is the team's evolving plan for achieving it.

### Lesson 6 — Daily Scrum, Teamwork & Blockers
Teach:
- Daily Scrum / stand-up,
- progress toward the Sprint Goal,
- blockers/impediments,
- transparency,
- accountability,
- concise professional communication,
- collaboration.

Classic beginner prompts may be introduced:
- What did I accomplish?
- What am I working on?
- What is blocking me?

Do not present these questions as a rigid requirement of Scrum.

Application:
Students analyze a fictional stand-up and identify appropriate next actions.

### Lesson 7 — Sprint Review, Retrospective & Improvement
Teach:
- Sprint Review,
- stakeholder feedback,
- inspecting the product,
- adapting the backlog,
- Sprint Retrospective,
- inspecting the team's process,
- continuous improvement,
- responding to change.

Application:
Students review the fictional team's sprint results, react to client feedback, update priorities, and identify one improvement for the team's next sprint.

### Lesson 8 — Putting Agile Into Practice
Teach/review:
- complete Agile/Scrum workflow,
- how the major concepts fit together,
- how students will use Agile in future development projects.

Application:
Students work through a final software-team scenario and complete a mostly auto-graded assessment.

End the module by transitioning students into actual use of Agile during future software-development projects.

## Career Essentials Integration

Career-readiness skills should be embedded rather than taught as disconnected soft-skill lessons.

Connections:

- User stories → customer focus and communication
- Backlog prioritization → decision-making
- Sprint planning → planning and time management
- Task ownership → accountability
- Daily Scrum → professional communication
- Blockers → problem solving and asking for help
- Reviews → receiving and responding to feedback
- Retrospectives → self-reflection and continuous improvement
- Changing requirements → adaptability
- Development teamwork → collaboration

## Agile Project Toolkit for Future Development Projects

After students complete the module, future software projects should reuse a lightweight Agile process:

1. Project Goal
2. User Stories
3. Product Backlog
4. Sprint Goal
5. Sprint Backlog / Task Board
6. Development check-ins
7. Sprint Review
8. Retrospective

Do not let Agile documentation overwhelm programming time. The process should support development, not become the project itself.

## Tone and Formatting

Use:
- clear headings,
- concise explanations,
- realistic software examples,
- student-friendly language,
- predictable lesson structure,
- short chunks appropriate for asynchronous learning.

Avoid:
- excessive emojis,
- overly childish language,
- long walls of text,
- unnecessary motivational filler,
- fake workplace jargon,
- worksheets dominated by manual writing,
- inaccurately rigid descriptions of Scrum.
